Anatomy of a Cheater-Proof Room:
Deconstructing the Technologies of AI-
Powered Proctoring
Core Architectural Frameworks for Proctoring Systems
The architectural design of modern AI-powered interview proctoring systems represents a
sophisticated response to the dual pressures of ensuring absolute security and
maintaining a functional, privacy-respecting user experience. The predominant model
that has emerged to address these competing demands is a Hybrid Edge-Cloud
architecture  . This framework strategically partitions computational tasks between the
candidate's local device (the "edge") and a centralized cloud backend, creating a
symbiotic relationship that optimizes for low latency, robust security, and scalable
analytics. The rationale for this model is rooted in the fundamental requirements of real-
time monitoring, where every millisecond of delay can impact both the candidate's
experience and the system's ability to respond to anomalies. By executing the most time-
sensitive components locally, these systems achieve responsiveness unattainable with
purely server-side processing. Simultaneously, they leverage the cloud for orchestration,
heavy-duty computation, and long-term data aggregation, thereby balancing performance
with centralized control and advanced analytical capabilities.
The client-side, or edge, component is the cornerstone of the system's real-time defense.
It is here that computationally intensive computer vision (CV) inference tasks are
executed directly within the candidate's web browser . This approach is critical for
achieving the stringent latency requirements necessary for live monitoring; target
latencies for CV inference are often below 200 milliseconds to ensure that the proctoring
system remains synchronized with the video stream and can react instantaneously to
suspicious behavior . This local execution is made possible through the adoption of
modern web standards like WebAssembly (Wasm) and WebGPU. These technologies
allow complex machine learning models, originally developed in languages like C++ or
Python, to be compiled and run efficiently on the client's CPU and GPU without requiring
plugins or native code installation . For instance, TensorFlow.js provides a JavaScript
library that enables developers to run and even retrain ML models in the browser, while
ONNX Runtime Web facilitates the deployment of models from various frameworks,62

A paramount benefit of this edge-centric approach is its profound impact on data privacy
and regulatory compliance. By performing initial analysis locally, the system can process
raw biometric data—such as facial images and eye movements—without ever
transmitting it to a remote server . Instead of sending a continuous, high-bandwidth
video feed, only lightweight, structured telemetry data is sent over a secure WebSocket
connection . This payload might contain JSON objects with timestamped information
about gaze vectors, head pose angles, or the confidence level of a detected gesture . This
strategy of data minimization is not merely a best practice but a necessity for complying
with increasingly strict global biometric privacy laws such as the General Data Protection
Regulation (GDPR) in Europe, the Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA) in Illinois,
USA, and the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) . These regulations often impose
stringent rules on the collection, storage, and use of biometric identifiers. By keeping raw
video streams local and only transmitting anonymized or hashed biometric templates
(like a cryptographic hash of a facial vector), proctoring platforms can significantly
mitigate their legal and ethical liabilities . Furthermore, this architecture enhances
user trust, as candidates are more likely to consent to a system that processes sensitive
data on their own device rather than on a third-party server.
The server-side, or cloud, component serves as the central nervous system for the entire
platform. Its responsibilities are primarily focused on orchestration, data aggregation,
and large-scale analytics. When a candidate initiates an interview session, the cloud
backend handles authentication, session management, and the generation of secure,
time-limited tokens required for establishing connections, such as those needed for
WebRTC-based video streaming . Once the session begins, the cloud becomes the
destination for the high-throughput telemetry stream sent from the client. To manage this
influx of data—especially from multiple concurrent interviews—a scalable messaging
queue like Apache Kafka is often employed . Kafka acts as a buffer, allowing the backend
to ingest vast amounts of real-time data from various sources without being
overwhelmed. This data is then processed by dedicated microservices, typically built with
frameworks like FastAPI in Python, which are designed to handle specific analytical
tasks . These services perform complex operations such as aggregating biometric data
over time, applying sophisticated anomaly detection algorithms, and calculating the final
"Integrity Score" for each session . While the edge device performs immediate, real-time
checks, the cloud is responsible for the holistic analysis that informs the recruiter's final
review.62

This division of labor also provides a crucial layer of resilience and scalability. If a
candidate's device lacks the resources to run the CV models at the desired frame rate
(e.g., dropping below 15 FPS), the client-side logic can detect this and signal the cloud
for fallback processing . In such scenarios, more computationally expensive models could
be invoked on powerful GPU-accelerated nodes within the cloud infrastructure to
continue monitoring the session, albeit with slightly higher latency . This graceful
degradation ensures that the proctoring function is maintained even on less capable
hardware. The cloud infrastructure itself is typically deployed on a Kubernetes cluster

partitioned based on data type. Video recordings and artifacts are securely stored in

InfluxDB, which is optimized for handling massive volumes of timestamped data

for each data type, ensuring optimal performance and cost-efficiency. The combination of
these elements creates a robust, secure, and highly scalable architectural foundation
upon which the advanced monitoring capabilities of modern proctoring systems are built.

Advanced Gesture and Pose Recognition for Physical
Integrity
The physical integrity of an online interview hinges on the system's ability to verify that
the candidate is alone, unaided, and engaged with the assessment in real-time. Advanced
gesture and pose recognition systems serve as the primary sensor for this dimension of
proctoring, employing a suite of computer vision techniques to monitor the candidate's
body language and environment. These systems move far beyond simple face detection,
analyzing skeletal structures to identify a wide range of actions and objects that could
indicate academic dishonesty. The technology stack for this capability is dominated by
several key frameworks and methodologies, each contributing to a multi-layered defense
against common cheating vectors.
At the forefront of this domain is Google's MediaPipe , a cross-platform framework
frequently cited for its efficiency and high-performance implementations of various
machine learning pipelines, particularly for human body and facial analysis . For
pose estimation, MediaPipe Pose is a top-tier solution that detects 33 key points of the
human body, including joints like the shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, and ankles 
. This detailed skeletal map allows the system to track the candidate's posture, head
orientation, and limb movements with remarkable precision. MediaPipe supports multi-
person pose detection, a critical feature for identifying a second person in the room. By
configuring the num_poses  parameter in the PoseLandmarkerOptions , the system
can be instructed to detect up to four distinct individuals within a single frame . Each
detected individual is represented as a separate list of keypoints, enabling the software to
differentiate between the candidate and any unauthorized personnel who may have
entered the camera's field of view . The system analyzes the number of tracked
poses; if more than one full set of landmarks is consistently detected, it triggers a high-
severity flag for a secondary person . MediaPipe is particularly well-suited for browser-
based applications because its models are optimized to run efficiently on CPUs and GPUs
via WebGL, making it a cornerstone of Edge AI proctoring solutions .
Another prominent framework is OpenPose , an open-source system renowned for its
bottom-up approach to multi-person pose estimation . Unlike some top-down methods
that first detect people and then analyze each one, OpenPose first identifies all individual
body parts (e.g., hands, feet, faces) across the entire image and then uses Part Affinity
Fields (PAFs)—a representation of how body parts are connected—to group them into
complete skeletons for each person . This makes OpenPose exceptionally effective at
handling crowded scenes with multiple overlapping individuals, offering a different1965

In addition to full-body pose estimation, many systems incorporate YOLO (You Only
Look Once)  object detection models, with YOLOv8  being a popular choice, to scan for
specific prohibited items . This is typically implemented in a two-stage pipeline.
First, the YOLOv8 model, which is highly efficient and accurate for object detection, scans
the video frame to draw bounding boxes around any objects it recognizes as "restricted,"
such as mobile phones, books, or laptops . Second, once a restricted object is
located, a more specialized module, such as a traditional face detector like Dlib or a
cropped version of the main pose estimator, can be applied to determine if a human hand
is interacting with the object . This modular approach combines the speed of YOLOv8
with the precision of other models, creating a robust system for detecting the
unauthorized use of aids. The integration of object detection is a critical layer of defense,
as it directly addresses the threat of candidates having access to hidden notes or
electronic devices.
Beyond object detection, the system meticulously monitors specific gestures and
movements that are indicative of cheating. One of the most commonly flagged gestures is
the hand-to-ear motion , which could signify the use of a concealed Bluetooth earpiece
to receive instructions or answers . MediaPipe includes a dedicated Hand tracking
module that provides 21 landmarks for each hand, allowing the system to precisely track
finger and wrist positions . By analyzing the trajectory of the hand's keypoints, the
software can detect when a hand moves rapidly towards the ear and raise a
corresponding alert . Another critical area of monitoring is the candidate's head pose.
Continuous tracking of head yaw (left-right rotation), pitch (up-down tilt), and roll
(tilting of the head sideways) is standard practice . If the candidate turns their head
beyond a predefined threshold, such as 45 degrees, the system can automatically mute
the microphone and display a non-punitive warning overlay, prompting the candidate to
refocus on the screen . This prevents the candidate from looking away to consult external
materials. The combination of these techniques—multi-person pose detection for
secondary individuals, object detection for unauthorized aids, and gesture tracking for
prohibited actions—creates a comprehensive physical surveillance layer that is essential
for securing the interview environment.20

Multi-Dimensional Eye Tracking and Pupil-Level
Analysis
While gesture and pose recognition provide a robust measure of physical presence and
action, advanced eye tracking delves deeper into the cognitive and physiological states of
the candidate, offering a more nuanced form of behavioral biometrics. Modern proctoring
systems leverage sophisticated algorithms to interpret subtle eye movements and
pupillary changes, moving beyond simple gaze direction to infer intent, cognitive load,
and biological liveness. This dimension of monitoring is powered by a combination of
foundational computer vision models and specialized analytical techniques that transform
raw video data into actionable insights. The ultimate goal is to create a multi-dimensional
profile of the candidate's visual behavior, which can be correlated with established
patterns of cheating.
The technological bedrock for most browser-based eye tracking systems is Google's
MediaPipe FaceMesh . This framework is a high-fidelity facial landmark detection
model capable of running in real-time on-device, making it ideal for Edge AI
implementations . FaceMesh identifies and tracks 468 three-dimensional21

From the foundational gaze data provided by FaceMesh, the system derives several
critical metrics related to eye movement. One of the most important is the analysis of 
fixations and saccades . Fixations are brief periods (typically lasting 100-400
milliseconds) where the eyes are relatively still, allowing for information processing .
Saccades are the rapid, ballistic movements that shift the gaze from one point of fixation
to another . Proctoring systems employ sophisticated algorithms to classify these
events based on spatial and temporal thresholds, identifying patterns that deviate from
normal, context-appropriate behavior . For example, prolonged off-screen gazes
lasting longer than a predefined duration (e.g., 3 seconds) are often flagged as
suspicious, as they may indicate the candidate is looking at external aids . Similarly,
erratic or excessively frequent saccades, or abnormal blinking patterns, can be indicative
of stress or an attempt to evade tracking . Certain gaze patterns are strongly associated
with cheating; for instance, sustained glances towards the top-right corner of the screen
are often interpreted as a sign of referencing hidden notes or a second screen, a behavior
known as "looking up and to the right" .
Perhaps the most powerful application of pupil-level analysis is the measurement of the 
Pupillary Light Reflex (PLR) , a cornerstone of active liveness detection . The PLR is
an involuntary physiological response where the pupil constricts in response to an
increase in light intensity and dilates when light levels decrease . Proctoring systems
exploit this reflex to definitively prove the presence of a living human being, effectively
neutralizing photo and video replay attacks . During an active check, the system
rapidly cycles the background color or brightness of the interview interface on the screen.
It then uses the on-device camera and MediaPipe FaceMesh to continuously measure the
change in the candidate's pupil diameter in response to these artificial light shifts .
A valid biological response—where the pupil contracts sharply in a bright flash and
dilates in a dark transition—is a strong indicator of a real, living person. The absence of3955

An even more advanced technique employed for liveness verification is Remote
Photoplethysmography (rPPG) . This cutting-edge, non-contact method extracts a user's
heartbeat from a standard video stream by analyzing minute, cyclical variations in skin
color caused by blood flow . As blood pulses through the capillaries near the skin's
surface, it causes subtle changes in the amount of red light absorbed and reflected, which
are imperceptible to the naked eye but can be captured by a camera . rPPG algorithms
isolate these micro-variations in the RGB channels of the video feed over time and apply
signal processing techniques, such as Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), to extract the heart
rate signal . Because photos, masks, and even some deepfakes lack a realistic, pulsatile
blood flow, the successful detection of a heartbeat via rPPG serves as a powerful proof of
life . Research has demonstrated the feasibility of integrating rPPG into face anti-
spoofing systems, with some approaches using Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) to
improve the accuracy of the measured signals . The combination of gaze tracking,
saccade analysis, PLR, and rPPG creates a comprehensive suite of tools for monitoring the
candidate's visual and physiological state, providing a formidable defense against a wide
array of impersonation and assisted cheating techniques.

Environmental Isolation and Auditory Forensics
Creating a truly secure and isolated digital environment is arguably the most formidable
challenge for online proctoring systems, given the inherent openness of web browsers
and operating systems. A candidate's device is a complex ecosystem, and preventing
unauthorized interactions requires a multi-pronged strategy that leverages browser APIs,
clever workarounds, and continuous auditory monitoring. The goal is to establish a
"digital lockdown" that prevents candidates from accessing external resources,
communicating with others, or using auxiliary displays to gain an unfair advantage. This
dimension of security relies heavily on the interplay between the proctoring application
and the underlying browser and operating system, pushing the boundaries of what is
technically feasible within a web context.
A primary method for controlling the candidate's immediate workspace is through the
enforcement of full-screen mode . The proctoring application requests exclusive
permission to display the interview in full screen, a standard browser capability designed
to immerse users in a webpage without distractions . This action effectively hides
browser UI elements like the address bar and tabs, reducing the temptation and
opportunity for the candidate to switch contexts. However, simply entering full-screen
mode is not enough, as users can often exit it by pressing the Esc key or using other OS-
level shortcuts. Therefore, the system must continuously monitor the state of the
application. This is achieved primarily through the Page Visibility API , a critical browser
API for environmental control . This API provides events, specifically the 
visibilitychange  event, which fires whenever the document's visibility state changes
—for example, when the user switches to a different browser tab, minimizes the window,
or navigates to another application . When the document's visibility status becomes
"hidden," the proctoring software can immediately log the event, issue a warning to the
candidate, and potentially escalate it to a formal risk flag . This real-time feedback
loop is the cornerstone of detecting attempts to multitask or access external information.
To further harden the environment, proctoring systems implement measures to block
common avenues for cheating. Custom JavaScript can be used to intercept and disable
right-click context menus, preventing candidates from easily opening browser developer
tools, which could be used to inspect page elements or inject malicious scripts .
Similarly, text selection and copy-paste functions can be programmatically disabled to
stop candidates from copying questions or prompts to search engines . These client-
side controls, combined with the Page Visibility API, create a baseline of environmental
isolation. However, the rise of modern browser extension standards, particularly 
Manifest V3 , has introduced new complexities. Manifest V3 imposes stricter permissions22

While browser controls manage the digital interface, auditory forensics  extend the
monitoring to the candidate's physical surroundings. Microphones are continuously
monitored throughout the interview to detect sounds that are inconsistent with a solo
examination. The primary concern is the detection of a secondary voice  speaking to or
guiding the candidate . Audio analysis algorithms can be trained to distinguish
between the candidate's voice and other sounds in the room, flagging instances of
conversation or whispered assistance . More advanced systems employ speaker
verification  techniques to confirm that any detected voice belongs to the registered
candidate. Methods like Gaussian Mixture Models with Universal Background Models
(GMM-UBM) or i-vector based systems can create a unique vocal signature of the
candidate and compare it against any other voices detected in the audio stream .
Another key area of audio analysis is the detection of keyboard typing . The sound of
typing can be analyzed to see if its cadence and rhythm match the candidate's natural
speech patterns. A mismatch, such as fast, rhythmic typing while the candidate is silent,
could suggest they are reading from a script or receiving real-time answers from someone
else . Some systems go so far as to use microphone arrays to perform active speaker
detection, triangulating the horizontal position of a talker to determine if they are located
at a desk (candidate) or elsewhere in the room (potential helper) . This
combination of digital lockdown measures and sophisticated audio analysis forms a

System Implementation, Performance, and Security
The successful implementation of a secure AI proctoring system depends not only on the
sophistication of its constituent technologies but also on the meticulous engineering of its
data pipeline, adherence to strict performance benchmarks, and a robust security posture.
The entire architecture must be designed to operate reliably under real-world conditions,
balancing demanding computational loads with the need for minimal latency and
maximum data protection. This involves careful selection of communication protocols,
data serialization formats, and encryption standards, all orchestrated to create a seamless
yet vigilant monitoring environment.
The communication between the client-side proctoring agent and the cloud backend is a
critical performance bottleneck that must be carefully managed. To facilitate real-time
interaction, the system utilizes WebRTC  for establishing the high-fidelity, low-latency22

audio and video stream between the candidate and the interviewer . For the continuous
telemetry data generated by the CV models (e.g., gaze vectors, head pose, gesture
confidences), a WebSocket  connection is the preferred protocol . WebSockets provide a
persistent, bidirectional channel over which the client can push small, frequent updates
to the server without the overhead of repeated HTTP requests. To optimize bandwidth
consumption, which is vital for candidates with limited internet connectivity, this
telemetry data is serialized using an efficient binary format like Protocol Buffers
(Protobuf)  or MessagePack  instead of plain JSON . These formats offer significant
compression over text-based representations, helping to keep the total data payload
under a target of 50 KB/s . On the server side, this high-volume stream of telemetry is
ingested by a message broker like Apache Kafka, which decouples the data producers
(clients) from the data consumers (backend processing workers), ensuring that the
system can handle bursts of activity and scale horizontally as needed .
Performance is a non-negotiable requirement, with strict benchmarks defined for both
the client and server sides. On the client, the primary metric is the frame rate  at which
the CV models can process the video stream. The system must be able to process video at
a minimum of 30 frames per second (FPS) locally . If the FPS drops below a critical
threshold of 15 FPS due to insufficient client hardware, the system should alert the user
to close background applications to free up resources, or gracefully degrade the
monitoring strictness . Latency is equally critical; the round-trip time for a CV inference
and the transmission of its results must remain below 200 milliseconds to ensure the
proctoring is perceived as instantaneous and does not desynchronize with the video feed 
. On the server side, the key benchmark is uptime , which must be maintained at
99.99% to ensure business continuity and reliability for recruiters and candidates alike .
This requires a resilient cloud infrastructure built on technologies like Kubernetes with
auto-scaling groups and redundant components to handle failures gracefully.
Data security and privacy are paramount concerns in a system that handles sensitive
biometric and personal information. The implementation plan must embed security at
every layer of the architecture. All data transmitted between the client and server must
be encrypted in transit using a strong protocol like TLS 1.3  . At rest, sensitive data such
as candidate videos and raw biometric logs must be stored in encrypted object storage
(e.g., AWS S3 with SSE-KMS) and databases, respectively . A crucial principle is data
minimization . Raw, unprocessed video streams containing biometric data should never
leave the candidate's device unless absolutely necessary . Instead, the system should
transmit only the final, aggregated telemetry data or anonymized risk flags. For
personally identifiable information (PII) like names and emails, end-to-end encryption
should be applied at the application layer before the data is even written to the relational
database . Biometric templates, such as the numerical vector representing a candidate's12

high-frequency telemetry data after a predetermined period, such as 90 days, retaining
only the essential metadata and aggregated results for historical purposes .

Synthesis: Integrity Scoring and Navigating Systemic
Risks
The culmination of the diverse technologies discussed—from gesture recognition and eye
tracking to environmental isolation and auditory forensics—is the creation of a unified,
multi-modal integrity score. This score, typically presented on a scale of 0 to 100, is the
primary output for recruiters, synthesizing a vast amount of disparate data into a single,12
7

=== PAGE 14 ===

actionable metric that reflects the likelihood of academic dishonesty during an interview .
The calculation of this score is not a simple average but a sophisticated aggregation
performed by an integrity scoring engine, which weighs different types of risk events
based on their severity and context . For example, a flag for a secondary person in the
room would carry more weight than a brief moment of off-screen gaze. This fusion of
passive, continuous monitoring with periodic active checks creates a much more resilient
system than any single modality could achieve alone. The resulting score, coupled with a
detailed timeline of timestamped risk events, empowers recruiters to make informed
judgments without having to manually review hours of video, transforming a
cumbersome forensic task into an efficient, evidence-based review process .
However, the implementation of such a powerful surveillance system is fraught with
systemic risks that must be proactively managed. The most significant challenge is
avoiding a high rate of false positives . Overly aggressive algorithms, particularly in
computer vision, can misinterpret nervous habits, medical conditions, or simple user
errors as cheating. A candidate who blinks frequently, twitches, or needs to look away to
think might be incorrectly flagged, leading to a negative assessment and potential
reputational damage . To mitigate this, a multi-layered mitigation strategy is essential.
First, the system should incorporate contextual awareness; for instance, instead of
immediately flagging a candidate who looks away, it should first trigger a gentle, non-
punitive UI prompt asking them to refocus . Second, a human-in-the-loop review process
should be mandatory for any session that receives a low integrity score, ensuring that
automated flags are validated by a person before any consequences are applied . Third,
the system should offer an "Accommodation Mode," which can be activated based on
documented medical needs (e.g., for conditions like Tourette's syndrome or severe
anxiety), temporarily disabling sensitive modules like gaze and micro-expression tracking
to ensure fairness and accessibility .
Another major risk is algorithmic bias . Machine learning models used for facial
recognition, pose estimation, and liveness detection are trained on large datasets, and if
these datasets are not diverse and representative, the models can exhibit significant
performance disparities across different demographics . For example, a facial recognition
model might have a lower accuracy rate for individuals with darker skin tones or those
who wear glasses, leading to higher false rejection rates for these groups. To combat this,
the development process must include a rigorous bias and fairness audit  phase. This
involves testing the CV models extensively across a wide range of candidates with varying
skin tones, ages, genders, and ethnicities under different lighting conditions . The goal is
to ensure that the false positive and false negative rates are consistent across all
demographic groups. Transparency in this process is also crucial; publishing the results of
these audits can help build trust with users and stakeholders.4

=== PAGE 15 ===

Finally, the project faces the risk of privacy backlash and legal challenges . The very
nature of collecting and analyzing biometric data is controversial, and public perception
can turn negative if not handled with extreme care . There is a delicate balance
between the need for security and the candidate's right to privacy. The mitigation
strategy here is to prioritize privacy by design. By leveraging an Edge-Cloud architecture
that processes and analyzes data locally on the candidate's device wherever possible, the
system minimizes the amount of raw biometric data that ever touches the cloud .
Engaging legal counsel specializing in biometric privacy laws (like BIPA and GDPR) from
the earliest stages of development is non-negotiable . Clear, transparent privacy policies
that explain exactly what data is collected, why it is needed, and how it is protected are
essential for obtaining and maintaining user consent. Ultimately, the success of a
proctoring system will depend not only on its technical prowess but also on its ability to
navigate these complex ethical, social, and legal landscapes responsibly.
