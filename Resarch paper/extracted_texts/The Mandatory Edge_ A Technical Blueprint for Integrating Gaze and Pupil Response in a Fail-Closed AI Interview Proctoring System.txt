Architectural Foundation for Mandatory Compliance
The implementation of a mandatory dual-modal biometric system hinges on an
architectural design that makes proctoring an inseparable and non-disruptable
component of the interview experience itself. This is not achieved through simple
software flags but through a deeply integrated, fail-closed framework where the
proctoring logic runs on the client-side, directly within the browser environment . The
core of this enforcement strategy lies in leveraging modern web technologies, specifically
WebAssembly (Wasm) and WebGPU, to execute complex computer vision (CV) models
locally . This approach ensures that any attempt to circumvent the system—by
closing the browser tab, switching applications, or disabling the camera—results in an
immediate and definitive halt to the interview session. The system's mandatoriness is thus
enforced by design, making the proctoring engine a fundamental part of the application's
runtime state. The initial Product Requirements Document (PRD) correctly identifies the
need for such a system, outlining functional requirements like continuous passive eye
tracking and active liveness challenges, while the Technical Requirements Document
(TRD) specifies the underlying technology stack necessary to achieve them .
The primary technical enabler for this mandatory architecture is the use of WebAssembly
and WebGPU for client-side inference . These technologies allow developers to
compile high-performance machine learning models, originally written in languages like
C++ or Python, into a format that can run at near-native speed inside a web browser 
. The TRD mandates that real-time CV inference must operate with less than 200ms
latency on the client side, a performance target that was once unattainable in the browser
but is now feasible due to the computational power offered by WebGPU . Benchmarks
show that WebGPU can accelerate ML workloads by over 30 times compared to CPU-
based Wasm execution, enabling the processing of high-resolution video streams from a
standard webcam at 30 frames per second (FPS) or more . This local processing499

Implementation of Real-Time Gaze Tracking
The implementation of real-time gaze tracking is a multi-stage process that transforms
raw video data from a standard webcam into a continuous stream of precise, actionable
data about a candidate's point of regard . This modality serves as the behavioral layer
of the dual-system, capturing voluntary eye movements indicative of attention,
distraction, or deception. The technical workflow, as synthesized from extensive research,
involves a sequence of well-defined computer vision and machine learning tasks, each
building upon the last to produce a robust gaze estimation model . The ultimate
goal is to meet the stringent accuracy and latency requirements outlined in the TRD,
ensuring the data is both reliable and usable in real-time without disrupting the interview
flow . The system must be capable of operating effectively under typical home office6191105

The foundational step in any gaze tracking pipeline is the accurate detection and
modeling of the human face and eyes . This is typically achieved using pre-trained
deep learning models optimized for speed and accuracy on consumer-grade hardware.
The MediaPipe Face Mesh solution is a prime example, capable of detecting 468
landmarks on the face in real-time using a standard RGB webcam . These
landmarks provide a stable geometric reference frame, precisely locating the boundaries
of the eyes, nose, and mouth. From this, the system can isolate the region of interest—the
eyes—which is crucial for the subsequent stages of pupil detection and gaze calculation.
This initial detection phase is computationally intensive but benefits immensely from
client-side acceleration via WebGPU, allowing it to run smoothly even on mid-range
hardware . Once the eyes are located, the next critical step is the precise localization of
the pupil's center . While early methods relied on simple heuristics like finding the
darkest spot in the iris region, modern approaches leverage deep learning. Models like
MediaPipe Iris or custom-built CNNs can accurately segment the pupil from the
surrounding sclera, even under challenging lighting conditions or when partially
obscured by eyelids . The output of this stage is a pixel coordinate representing the
center of the pupil within the captured image of the eye.
However, knowing the pupil's position relative to the eyeball is insufficient for
determining where a person is looking on a screen. Head movements introduce
significant noise into the calculation. A person can turn their head while keeping their
gaze fixed straight ahead, or they can move their eyes to look sideways while keeping
their head still. To disambiguate these cases, the system must simultaneously estimate
the three-dimensional orientation of the head relative to the camera, known as head pose
estimation . Using the 3D coordinates of the facial landmarks detected by
MediaPipe, the system can calculate the Euler angles for pitch (up-down tilt), yaw (left-
right rotation), and roll (tilting of the ear towards the shoulder) . This head pose
information is used to normalize the raw eye orientation data, correcting for the effect of
head movement and isolating the true gaze direction of the eyeball itself. This fusion of
head pose and eye orientation data is a common technique in appearance-based gaze
estimation systems . The final piece of the puzzle is calibration, a crucial procedure
required to map the normalized eye and head orientation data to a specific point-of-gaze
(PoG) on the computer screen . Without calibration, the system knows the direction
of the gaze vector but not where it intersects the screen . The most common
calibration method involves presenting the user with a series of targets at known
locations on the screen and recording the corresponding eye/head data while they fixate428

Implementation of Pupillary Light Reflex Analysis
The second mandatory modality, pupillary light reflex (PLR) analysis, provides the
physiological layer of the dual-system, measuring involuntary, subconscious responses to
visual stimuli . Unlike gaze tracking, which measures voluntary behavior, PLR is
governed by the autonomic nervous system and is extremely difficult to consciously
control or fake . This makes it an exceptionally powerful indicator of genuine
engagement with the screen content. The implementation of PLR analysis requires a
tightly coupled system of stimulus presentation and high-frequency response
measurement, both executed within the client-side browser environment. The goal is to
programmatically induce the reflex and then capture the subtle changes in pupil diameter
with sufficient precision to extract meaningful diagnostic parameters . This
component directly addresses the challenge of verifying that a candidate's eyes are
genuinely interacting with the digital display, forming a crucial check against
sophisticated spoofing attacks.
The technical workflow for PLR analysis begins with the controlled presentation of a
visual stimulus designed to elicit the reflex . The PLR is triggered by changes in light
intensity, so the system must dynamically alter the brightness of the screen . This can
be accomplished in several ways. A simple yet effective method is to rapidly flash the
entire screen between a bright white state and a dark state, creating a clear, repeatable
stimulus cycle . More advanced implementations might involve changing the color or
intensity of specific UI elements, such as buttons or question fields, to create localized
stimuli . The timing and properties of these stimuli (e.g., duration, intensity) must be
logged with high precision, often synchronized with the system clock, as PLR parameters
like latency are measured relative to the exact moment of stimulus onset . The
accuracy of this stimulus presentation is paramount, as errors can invalidate the
subsequent analysis. The system should aim for stimulus changes that are rapid enough
to reliably trigger the reflex but not so fast as to cause visual discomfort or flicker fusion.
Standardized guidelines for stimulus calibration, similar to those used in ophthalmic
instruments (e.g., ISCEV guidelines), can inform the design of these visual cues .
Concurrent with the stimulus presentation, the system must measure the pupil's response
with high temporal resolution. This is achieved using the same webcam and computer
vision pipeline as the gaze tracker. After the eye region is detected and the pupil
localized, the system tracks the radius of the pupil over time . Libraries like 
jeelizPupillometry  demonstrate the feasibility of running such real-time
pupillometry in the browser, leveraging the GPU for efficient computation . To capture
the dynamics of the PLR, which includes a relatively fast constriction phase and a slower268

unnatural state where the pupil is not reacting as it should . The ability to measure
these parameters remotely using a standard webcam has been demonstrated in several
studies, validating the core premise of this component . The integration of PLR
analysis with the gaze tracker creates a powerful synergy. While the gaze tracker
confirms where the candidate is looking, the PLR analysis confirms that their eyes are
physiologically reacting to what they see on the screen.

Integrated Fusion Logic for Anomaly Detection and
Liveness Verification
The true power and mandatory nature of the dual-modal system are realized not in the
individual capabilities of gaze tracking and pupillary light reflex (PLR) analysis, but in
their intelligent fusion. This integrated logic forms the analytical core of the system,
correlating behavioral and physiological signals to detect anomalies indicative of cheating
or spoofing. The primary enforcement mechanism is a correlation-based anomaly
detector that looks for statistically improbable combinations of data. The most salient
threat scenario—looking away from the screen to consult unauthorized materials—
creates a distinct signature that the fused system is uniquely equipped to identify: a
prolonged off-screen gaze accompanied by an absent pupillary light reflex to sustained
screen brightness . By requiring both modalities to be active and continuously cross-
validating their outputs, the system creates a defense-in-depth architecture that is far
more robust than either modality could be alone. This fusion logic is what elevates the
system from a collection of sensors to a coherent, intelligent proctor.
The foundation of this fusion logic is the establishment of a personalized baseline for
each candidate during the initial setup and calibration phase . This baseline captures the
individual's unique physiological and behavioral characteristics under normal, honest
conditions. The system records several key metrics: the candidate's average resting pupil
diameter under the ambient room lighting, their typical PLR latency and constriction
velocity when presented with a standardized light stimulus, and their general gaze
patterns when focusing on neutral content like the interviewer's video feed . These
baselines are not static; they are established for the specific environmental conditions of
the interview, particularly the lighting, which is a critical variable for both pupil size and9586

PLR measurement . With this baseline established, the system can begin real-time

During the interview, the fusion engine continuously monitors the two data streams. The
primary pattern it hunts for is the "off-screen gaze + absent PLR" signature. The gaze
tracker reports the direction of the candidate's gaze. If this vector indicates the candidate
is looking off-screen (e.g., to the top-right corner, a common location for hidden notes)
for a duration exceeding a set threshold (e.g., 3 seconds), a low-level alert is generated 
. However, this by itself is not conclusive evidence of cheating, as a candidate might
legitimately look away to think. Here is where the PLR module provides the decisive
confirmation. The PLR analyzer is constantly monitoring the pupil diameter and the
screen's luminance. If the candidate's gaze is off-screen, but the screen's background
remains brightly lit, the system expects to see a PLR—the pupil should constrict in
response to the light. If the system observes that the pupil diameter remains large and
shows no signs of constriction, it constitutes strong corroborating evidence. The absence
of a physiological response when a response is expected is a powerful indicator of an
unnatural state, suggesting the candidate is not actually looking at the screen . The
simultaneous occurrence of these two events—an off-screen gaze from the behavioral
modality and an absent PLR from the physiological modality—triggers a high-confidence
violation. The system can then escalate this event to a recruiter for review or, according
to predefined policies, take automated actions like pausing the interview or terminating
the session .
Beyond detecting cheating, this dual-modality system is exceptionally effective for
liveness detection, a critical component of anti-spoofing . Spoofing attacks, such as
presenting a photograph, a recorded video, or a high-quality 3D mask of another person,
rely on static or pre-recorded representations. These representations inherently lack the
dynamic biological properties of a living person. The PLR analysis provides a direct
countermeasure. A static photo or a simple video replay will not exhibit a pupillary light
reflex; the pupil in the image will remain fixed regardless of changes in screen brightness 
. Furthermore, active liveness checks can be integrated into the workflow. The system
can randomly prompt the candidate to follow a moving dot on the screen with their eyes .
A successful response requires both valid gaze tracking (the eyes must move to follow the
dot) and a corresponding PLR (the pupil should react to the light changes inherent in the
animation). A 3D mask might replicate a static gaze but may struggle to perfectly mimic
the subtle micro-movements (saccades, blinks) and the full dynamic range of a real PLR,
especially under inconsistent lighting . The combination of passive, continuous
monitoring (correlating gaze with PLR) and periodic active challenges (requiring the
candidate to perform specific eye movements) creates multiple layers of defense against a519531

On the client side, the application is responsible for orchestrating the entire proctoring
workflow. It initializes the camera feed using WebRTC for low-latency streaming . The
core of the client-side application is the WebAssembly/WebGPU-powered inference
engine, which runs the CV models for gaze and PLR analysis in real-time . This
engine continuously processes video frames and generates a stream of telemetry data. To
manage network bandwidth and latency, this data is serialized into compact JSON
payloads and sent to the backend via a dedicated WebSocket endpoint, /ws/v1/
telemetry  . These payloads contain timestamped data points for gaze vectors, head
pose, and pupil diameter, compressed using an efficient format like Protobuf or
MessagePack to keep the data rate under 50 KB/s . The client application also manages
the UI flow, guiding the user through the mandatory onboarding, calibration, and
interview phases, and communicating system status and warnings back to the candidate .
The backend architecture is designed for scalability and reliability. At its heart is an API
Gateway (using GraphQL for flexible data fetching and REST for webhooks) that routes
incoming requests . Real-time telemetry from clients is ingested by a high-throughput
messaging system like Apache Kafka, which decouples the data producers (clients) from
the data consumers (processing services) and buffers the massive influx of data . This
allows the system to handle traffic spikes gracefully. Processing Workers, built with
Python and FastAPI, consume the telemetry streams from Kafka. These microservices are
responsible for a variety of tasks: writing the raw time-series data to a specialized
database, running more computationally intensive anomaly detection algorithms that
may not be suitable for the client-side, and aggregating data to calculate the final
Integrity Score . The Recruiter Dashboard is also served by the backend, which fetches
aggregated session data and risk events to populate its timeline and scoring interface. For
scenarios where client-side processing is too resource-intensive (e.g., older hardware),
the architecture must have a fallback to cloud-based processing, although this introduces
higher latency and should be avoided when possible . The entire backend is deployed on5961

Security, Privacy, and Compliance Framework
Implementing a dual-modal biometric proctoring system necessitates a rigorous security
and privacy framework, given the highly sensitive nature of the collected data. Biometric
information, including facial geometry, eye movements, and pupillary responses, is
considered Personally Identifiable Information (PII) and is protected under numerous
global regulations such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in Europe, the
Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA) in Illinois, and the California Consumer Privacy
Act (CCPA) . The system's design must therefore embed privacy and security by
default, adhering to a "Privacy-Protecting Techniques for Behavioral Biometric Data"
survey . The architecture must minimize data exposure, employ strong encryption, and
provide transparency and control to the user, all while upholding the highest industry
standards for authentication and anti-spoofing.
The core principle of data security is end-to-end protection. All biometric data must be
encrypted both in transit and at rest . Communication between the client browser and the
cloud backend must use TLS 1.3 to prevent eavesdropping . For data at rest, all databases
and storage systems must be encrypted using strong algorithms like AES-256 . A
particularly critical aspect is the handling of raw biometric templates, such as the
candidate's facial embedding vector used for identity verification. Instead of storing these
vectors in plaintext, the system should store only a secure cryptographic hash of the
vector in the relational database . This ensures that even if the database is
compromised, the original biometric template cannot be easily reconstructed. Raw video
recordings, which contain the most sensitive data, should ideally never leave the client's
device unless a high-confidence risk event is detected. Even then, access to these
recordings should be strictly controlled, granted only via short-lived, pre-signed URLs and
audited meticulously . Furthermore, all Personally Identifiable Information (PII) within
the application, such as candidate names and emails, should be masked or tokenized in
analytics dashboards and logs to prevent accidental exposure .
