Beyond the Screen: An Anatomy of AI
Interview Proctoring, Spoofing Evasion,
and Advanced Biometric Defenses
Core Technical Arsenal for Proctoring Automated
Interviews
The landscape of AI-powered interviewing, utilized by various technology and hiring
companies, is built upon a sophisticated suite of computer vision and machine learning
technologies designed to ensure assessment integrity . These systems move far beyond
simple transcription of answers, employing a multi-layered technical arsenal to monitor
candidate behavior in real-time. The primary objective is to detect any deviation from
authentic, solo participation, which could indicate cheating. This involves a continuous
stream of analysis focused on three key domains: eye gaze, head pose, and physical
gestures. While specific commercial platforms like HireVue, Pymetrics, and MyInterview
remain largely opaque about their proprietary scoring algorithms, public research and
system descriptions reveal a common technological foundation for proctoring . The
most prevalent method for direct monitoring is eye gaze tracking, which analyzes where
a candidate is looking to infer attention and potential off-screen assistance . Systems
generate heatmaps and track fixation sequences to visualize and quantify the distribution
of a candidate's gaze across the screen . Research indicates that students may look
away from their screen during exams, and this behavior can be detected and flagged by
the system . However, the understanding of how major platforms score interviews has
evolved; for instance, it is noted that many people incorrectly assume HireVue judges
them based on eye contact or facial expressions, when its current methodology is more
nuanced .
A systematic literature review covering studies from 2016 to 2022 identified eight major
features extracted from eye-tracking data for classification tasks, highlighting the depth
of this analysis . These features include pupil size, saccade (rapid eye movements),
fixation, velocity of eye rotation, blink characteristics, pupil position, electrooculography
(EOG) signals, and overall gaze point . Among these, "fixation"—the periods when the
eyes are relatively still and focused—was the most commonly used feature for3

Beyond eye gaze, head pose estimation is another critical component of the technical
arsenal. This technology monitors the yaw (side-to-side), pitch (up-and-down), and roll
(tilting the ear towards the shoulder) angles of a candidate's head . Its primary
function is to identify if a candidate is looking off-screen, which could imply they are
consulting unauthorized materials or receiving assistance from someone else in the room 
. One study reported achieving 87% accuracy in pose estimation using a deep learning
model called Attentive Net to solve the Perspective-n-Point (PNP) equation,
demonstrating the feasibility of precise head movement tracking . The ability to
analyze head movements provides crucial context; for example, one framework
demonstrated a positive correlation between head movements and extraversion, while
finding a negative correlation with neuroticism . Confident and relaxed individuals
were observed to exhibit more head movements, which was also linked to higher sales
abilities . By using significant changes in head movement angles as predictive features,
a model achieved approximately 80% accuracy in classifying these personality traits,
showcasing the richness of data available from head pose analysis . For extraction,
systems utilize libraries like OpenCV, 3DDFA, and mediapipe to detect facial landmarks
and calculate these three-dimensional rotation angles throughout the interview .
Hand and body gesture recognition complements gaze and head pose analysis by
providing a broader view of the candidate's physical environment and actions. This is
primarily accomplished using pose estimation models such as OpenPose and MediaPipe 
. These models create a skeletal representation of the person in the video feed,
identifying key body joints and tracking their movement over time. This allows the
system to monitor for a range of suspicious activities, including leaving the seat, handling
objects, or making hand gestures that might signal communication with an outside party 2

Liveness Detection and Spoofing Countermeasures
Before any meaningful behavioral analysis can occur, AI proctoring systems must first
establish a foundational layer of security: verifying that the entity being interviewed is a
genuine, live human. This is the purpose of liveness detection, a critical set of anti-
spoofing techniques designed to thwart attempts to impersonate a candidate using static
images (photo attacks), dynamic media like videos or screen recordings (video replay
attacks), or constructed masks (mask attacks) . Relying solely on passive facial3

Therefore, modern systems incorporate active and passive checks to confirm biological
liveliness. Passive methods analyze inherent properties of a live face, such as the subtle
micro-movements and natural texture variations that are absent in fakes. Active methods
require the candidate to perform specific actions, like following a moving object on the
screen with their eyes, to prove they are a live person responding in real-time .
One of the most promising and technically robust approaches to liveness detection
involves analyzing pupillary response. The human pupil exhibits complex, involuntary
reactions to both light and cognitive tasks, which are difficult for artificial stimuli to
replicate. A proposed Presentation Attack Detection (PAD) system leverages this by
presenting the user with visual stimuli that move along randomized trajectories . The
user is prompted to follow this moving stimulus with their gaze. The system then
captures and analyzes the user's pupillary motion in response to these controlled visual
cues . Impostors attempting to use a photograph or a static mask cannot produce the
correct physiological response, as their eyes lack the necessary neural pathways. This
method effectively distinguishes genuine users from impostors. In experiments evaluating
this technique, the system demonstrated exceptionally high detection rates. For 2D mask
attacks (e.g., a printed photo with holes), the system achieved a True Positive Rate (TPR)
of 99.0% at a 10% False Positive Rate (FPR). For more sophisticated 3D mask attacks
(constructed from the victim's own data), the TPR reached 99.4% under the same
conditions . This highlights the power of using pupillary dynamics as a liveness
indicator. However, the research explicitly notes a significant limitation: this particular
gaze-based approach does not protect against video replay attacks, where an attacker
plays a pre-recorded video of the legitimate user. This underscores the necessity of a
multi-modal defense strategy, as no single technique can address all potential spoofing
vectors .
Other research into liveness detection explores alternative methods based on facial
landmark analysis. One comparative study evaluated five different approaches, including
the analysis of facial landmarks projection ratio . Another project focused on liveness
detection through the analysis of fake face movement and the examination of the pupil 
. These methods aim to detect the unnatural rigidity or inconsistencies in a fake face's
structure or movement compared to a live one. Continuous user verification represents a
more advanced application of liveness principles. Instead of a one-time check at the
beginning of the session, this approach iteratively confirms the candidate's identity
throughout the entire interview . This is often achieved through multi-modal biometric
fusion, combining signals from different sources like face recognition, keystroke
dynamics, and touch-screen interactions. Such a fused approach was reported to achieve5

In addition to biometric verification, AI proctoring systems employ several other technical
measures to create a secure environment and prevent cheating. Browser lockdown is a
common feature that prevents candidates from switching tabs or accessing other
applications on their computer, thereby restricting their ability to search for answers
online or consult external documents . Port blocking is another measure that restricts
network access to certain ports, limiting the candidate's ability to connect to
unauthorized devices or networks . Mobile device detection is also a critical safeguard.

the system can scan the candidate's environment for the presence of phones or other
mobile devices . The detection process can involve identifying phones based on facial

to receive answers or cheat in some other way . These technical controls work in
concert with the biometric monitoring layers. While eye tracking and gesture analysis
observe the candidate's behavior, browser lockdown and port blocking enforce the rules

formidable barrier against many common forms of cheating. The table below summarizes

Candidate Evasion Techniques and System
Vulnerabilities
As AI proctoring systems have become more sophisticated, so too have the methods
employed by candidates to circumvent their technical defenses. This dynamic creates a
persistent arms race where developers must constantly innovate to close newly
discovered vulnerabilities. The evasion tactics can be broadly categorized into spoofing
attacks, behavioral manipulation, and the use of external aids. Understanding these
counter-strategies is crucial for developing more resilient systems and for setting realistic
expectations about the capabilities of current technology. One of the most fundamental
evasion techniques is spoofing, where an individual attempts to impersonate the
registered candidate. This includes "photo attacks," where a high-resolution image of the
candidate is displayed to fool the facial recognition system . More advanced are "2D
mask attacks," which involve a printed photograph of the candidate, sometimes with
holes cut out for the eyes and mouth, and "3D mask attacks," which are constructed from
the candidate's own data to create a more realistic replica . While some liveness
detection systems are highly effective against these, particularly 3D masks, the challenge
varies significantly depending on the context. For instance, the gaze-based liveness
detection system described in one study struggled with photo attack detection, especially
on smaller mobile phone screens . At a stringent 1% False Positive Rate setting, the
True Positive Rate for detecting photo attacks on a phone format dropped to as low as
16.1%, although performance improved with longer challenge durations . This
highlights a key vulnerability: the effectiveness of anti-spoofing measures can degrade in
less controlled environments or on lower-quality hardware.
Another category of evasion involves behavioral manipulation. A candidate might
attempt to pre-record their answers and then read from a script or notes, thus avoiding
the spontaneous interaction that AI systems are designed to assess . This tactic directly
undermines the platform's goal of evaluating real-time communication skills. To appear
more natural, a candidate might also engage in behavioral mimicry, consciously trying to
replicate the nonverbal cues that are believed to be positively correlated with desirable
traits, such as nodding, smiling, or maintaining steady eye contact . If the system's
algorithm is simplistic, it could be fooled into interpreting this mimicry as genuine
confidence or engagement. A more advanced form of this is hiring a "ghost writer" who
provides answers to the candidate in real-time through a hidden earpiece or other covert
means . This type of collusion is particularly difficult for purely visual systems to
detect, as the candidate's on-camera behavior might appear perfectly authentic while they
are receiving external assistance. This is why a multi-faceted approach that combines5

Advanced Behavioral Biometrics and Multi-Modal Data
Fusion
To build truly resilient AI interview systems, developers are increasingly turning to
advanced behavioral biometrics and the principle of multi-modal data fusion. This
approach moves beyond analyzing isolated behaviors like eye gaze or head nods and
instead seeks to construct a holistic, dynamic profile of the candidate by integrating data
from multiple sensors and channels. The underlying premise is that a single biometric
signal can be noisy, ambiguous, or vulnerable to spoofing. However, when combined
with other signals, the resulting composite profile becomes far more reliable and difficult
to manipulate. This integrated analysis allows the system to differentiate between
authentic, complex human behavior and the simpler, often more rigid patterns of
cheating or deception. The most sophisticated systems fuse data streams from various
sources, including but not limited to eye-tracking, head pose, facial expressions, hand
gestures, and even audio prosody .
Research in related fields provides strong evidence for the efficacy of this multi-modal
approach. A systematic literature review of 37 relevant articles found that the majority of
studies aiming for high performance combined eye-tracking data with other physiological
or behavioral signals . Electroencephalogram (EEG) was the most common
complementary signal, used in nine of the fourteen studies that integrated multiple
modalities . Other combinations included Galvanic Skin Response (GSR) for measuring
stress, functional MRI (fMRI) for brain activity mapping, and thermal imaging . While
fMRI is impractical for a standard interview setting, the principle holds: combining
different types of data provides a richer context for interpretation. In the context of
practical proctoring systems, this translates to fusing face recognition with other
continuous authentication methods like keystroke dynamics or mouse movements . A
system that verifies identity through a live human's face, then continues to monitor their
unique typing rhythm and mouse cursor patterns throughout the session, creates a much
stronger security posture than one relying on face recognition alone . This multi-modal
fusion was reported to achieve a high accuracy of 99.12% in continuous user verification,
demonstrating its power in maintaining session integrity .
Machine learning acts as the central engine that makes sense of this complex, multi-
channel data. Classifiers, particularly Support Vector Machines (SVM), are trained on
large datasets containing examples of both authentic candidate behavior and various
forms of cheating . The SVM learns to identify the subtle, complex patterns that
distinguish a nervous but honest candidate from a confident cheater. For instance, a237

The application of advanced biometrics extends the scope of analysis beyond simple
observation. By treating the candidate's behavior as a rich stream of data, the system can
infer deeper states like cognitive load, stress, and engagement. Features extracted from
eye-tracking, such as pupil size and blink rate, are known to correlate with mental effort
and fatigue . When combined with head pose data showing signs of restlessness and
audio data revealing a faster speech rate, the system can generate a more accurate
assessment of the candidate's state. This is not just about detecting cheating but also
about improving the quality of the assessment itself. For example, a candidate who
appears stressed might give suboptimal answers regardless of their qualifications.
Flagging this stress state could allow human reviewers to interpret the candidate's
performance in a more nuanced context. However, this advanced analysis also introduces
significant complexity and potential for error. The literature review on eye tracking
highlighted major inconsistencies in how researchers handle data gaps caused by blinks,
noting that poor data handling practices can lead to the loss of over 50% of usable data 
. This underscores a critical engineering challenge: ensuring the integrity of the raw
data streams before they are fed into the machine learning models. Without a robust pre-
processing pipeline that correctly handles artifacts and fills gaps using appropriate
methods like linear or cubic spline interpolation, the sophisticated analyses performed by
the ML classifiers will be based on flawed inputs, leading to unreliable and potentially
biased outcomes . Thus, the strength of any advanced behavioral biometric system
rests on two pillars: the quality and diversity of the fused data streams, and the rigor of
the data preprocessing and validation pipelines that support them.7

Systemic Challenges, Limitations, and Future
Trajectories
Despite the impressive sophistication of modern AI proctoring systems, they are not
without significant systemic challenges, limitations, and ethical considerations. A
thorough evaluation requires acknowledging the boundaries of current technology and
the potential consequences of its deployment. The most prominent gap in the public
domain is the lack of transparency from commercial vendors. Companies like HireVue,
Pymetrics, and others treat their specific algorithms, decision thresholds, and weighting
schemes for scoring interviews as proprietary trade secrets . While we can infer the
general types of technologies used based on academic research and system descriptions,
the exact mechanics of how a candidate receives a final score remain unknown . This
opacity makes it impossible for independent researchers, auditors, or even the candidates
themselves to fully understand or challenge the basis of an assessment. This black-box
nature of the algorithms raises profound questions about fairness, accountability, and the
potential for embedded biases.
Furthermore, there is a notable disconnect between the performance of these systems in
controlled laboratory settings versus their real-world efficacy. Many of the cited studies
report exceptionally high accuracy rates, such as 99% or more for liveness detection or
activity recognition . These figures are typically achieved under ideal conditions
with standardized equipment and carefully selected test subjects. The performance of
these systems in the wild, amidst diverse candidate demographics, varied home office
environments, different lighting conditions, and a wide array of potential evasion
techniques, is far less documented and likely to be lower. The risk of false positives is a
critical concern that is not adequately addressed in the provided sources. An algorithm
trained to detect stress or nervousness might unfairly penalize candidates from cultures
where direct eye contact is considered disrespectful, or those with medical conditions
that cause frequent blinking or tremors . Similarly, a model trained predominantly on
data from one demographic group may perform poorly or exhibit bias when applied to
another. The literature review on eye tracking, for instance, pointed out significant
shortcomings in how studies report their data handling procedures, which can introduce
bias and reduce reproducibility . Without rigorous, independent auditing for fairness
and bias, these systems risk automating and amplifying existing societal inequalities.
Looking forward, the trajectory of AI proctoring technology points toward greater
integration of physiological signals and the adoption of immersive environments. One
emerging frontier mentioned in the research is the integration of Extended Reality (XR)1

In synthesis, the technological battle over integrity in AI-powered interviews is a dynamic
arms race characterized by a cycle of innovation and evasion. The current state-of-the-art
relies on a multi-modal approach, fusing data from eye trackers, cameras, and other
sensors to build a comprehensive behavioral profile of the candidate. Foundational
security is provided by liveness detection mechanisms, such as pupillary response
analysis, designed to prevent impersonation. However, no single technology is infallible;
each comes with its own set of vulnerabilities, from the difficulty of detecting photo
attacks on small screens to the inability of gaze tracking to prevent video replay . The
true strength of modern systems lies in their resilience, achieved through the synergistic
combination of multiple, overlapping layers of security. The path forward will
undoubtedly involve continued advancements in machine learning, the exploration of
new data modalities like physiological signals and XR, and an increasing focus on
addressing the critical issues of bias, fairness, and transparency. For stakeholders—from
system developers and hiring managers to the candidates themselves—the central
takeaway is that while deliberate cheating is becoming increasingly difficult, the systems
are not perfect. Their ultimate value will depend not only on their technical prowess but
also on the responsible frameworks of oversight and accountability that govern their use.3
