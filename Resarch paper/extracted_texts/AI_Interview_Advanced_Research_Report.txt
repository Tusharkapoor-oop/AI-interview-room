1. The Neural Architecture of Real-Time Proctoring: F rom Pixels to Detec-
tion
1.1 MediaPipe: Google’s Multimodal Real-Time ML Pipeline
MediaPipe is not merely a collection of pre-trained models — it is a complete directed acyclic graph
(DAG) runtime designed for low-latency inference on streaming multimodal data. (Medium) The
framework models computation as a graph where each node is a Calculator (a self-contained processing
unit) that takes input streams and produces output streams. F or a hand-tracking pipeline, the execu-
tion graph flows as: Camera Input → Image T ransform → Palm Detection Model → Hand Landmark
Model → Render Output . Each Calculator runs independently , with the framework managing scheduling,
synchronization, and memory . While one frame is being rendered, the next frame is already being pro-
cessed by the detection model. This architectural design is what enables MediaPipe to achieve real-time
performance on CPU alone without GPU acceleration. (Medium)
The internal flow when calling hands.process(image) follows a strict five-stage pipeline: (1) Prepro-
cessing — the frame is resized, normalized, and converted to tensor format compatible with the model;
(2) Detection stage — a lightweight neural network (BlazeF ace, BlazePalm, etc.) locates regions of
interest (ROIs), such as palm bounding boxes; (3) Landmark regression — a second model runs on
each cropped ROI to predict precise landmark coordinates (21 for hands, 33 for body pose, 468 for face
mesh); (4) T racking optimization — on subsequent frames, if tracking confidence exceeds a threshold
(typically 0.5), detection is skipped entirely and previous landmarks are fed directly to the landmark
model, which is the mechanism behind the min_tracking_confidence parameter; and (5) Output pack-
aging — results are wrapped in Protocol Buffer objects and returned. This two-stage approach (detect
→ regress) plus frame-to-frame tracking is the key innovation that makes real-time proctoring feasible
on commodity hardware. (Medium)

1.3 F aceMesh and Iris: The Gaze T racking Pipeline
MediaPipe F aceMesh detects 468 dense facial landmarks including detailed eye region points, while
MediaPipe Iris specifically tracks iris, pupil, and eye contour landmarks using a single RGB camera.
(Google Research) The Iris model was trained on approximately 50,000 manually annotated images
representing diverse illumination conditions and head poses from geographically diverse regions. The
architecture uses a multi-task model with a unified encoder and separate components for eye contour
estimation and iris localization. (Google Research)
The depth estimation capability is particularly significant for proctoring: the model determines the
metric distance from camera to subject with less than 10% relative error without requiring
a dedicated depth sensor. This is achieved by exploiting the fact that the horizontal iris diameter
remains roughly constant at 11.7±0.5 mm across populations, combined with simple geometric
arguments from the pinhole camera model. The distance formula is: d = (real_iris_width × fo-
cal_length) / iris_width_pixels .(Google Research) When a candidate moves closer to or further
from the camera, this metric enables the system to normalize gaze estimates for distance variation —
a critical correction for proctoring scenarios where candidates may naturally shift position during long
interviews.

2.1 The Deviation Angle F ormula and Its Proctoring Application

camera center axis. The mathematical formulation uses the arccosine of the dot product: ￿ = arccos((G￿

2.3 Gaze Pattern Signatures of Cheating Behavior
The power of gaze tracking in proctoring lies not in detecting where a candidate looks, but in identifying
anomalous gaze patterns that correlate with specific cheating behaviors. When a person recalls
information from memory , their eyes typically drift upward or to the side (the “visual recall” pattern
documented in neurolinguistic programming research). When reading from a screen or notes, eyes move
in horizontal scanning patterns from left to right with quick snaps back to the start of each line — a
mechanical pattern fundamentally different from natural cognitive processing. (F abric) The system builds
atemporal profile of candidate behavior by collecting gaze data throughout the interview, computing
key metrics including gaze entropy (randomness in patterns), gaze path deviation from expected
attention areas, and temporal gaze correlation between eye movement and verbal response timing.

3. T emporal Modeling: TimeSformer and the F uture of Video-Based Proc-
toring
3.1 Divided Space-Time Attention Architecture
TimeSformer represents a fundamental departure from convolution-based video analysis by building ex-
clusively on self-attention over space and time .(ar5iv) The model views a video as a sequence of
frame-level patches (typically 16×16 pixels), where each patch is linearly mapped into an embedding
and augmented with positional information. This converts the video into a sequence of spatiotem-
porally ordered tokens analogous to word embeddings in NLP T ransformers. The key innovation is
“divided attention” : temporal attention and spatial attention are applied separately within each
block , with temporal attention comparing each patch only with patches at the same spatial location in
other frames, followed by spatial attention comparing each patch with patches within the same frame.
(ar5iv)
The computational advantage is dramatic. Joint space-time attention requires O(T × N) comparisons
per patch (where T is frames and N is patches per frame), while divided attention requires only O(T
+ N) comparisons. (meta.com) In practice, joint attention causes GPU memory overflow at 448-
pixel resolution or 32-frame inputs, while divided attention scales eﬀiciently to 96 frames spanning
102 seconds of video — a critical capability for analyzing long-form interview behavior. (meta.com)
TimeSformer achieves state-of-the-art results on Kinetics-400 and Kinetics-600 benchmarks, with
divided attention consistently outperforming all alternative space-time factorization schemes. (ar5iv)

3.2 F rom Action Recognition to Behavioral Anomaly Detection
While TimeSformer was originally designed for action recognition (identifying activities like “playing
basketball” or “making tea”), its architecture is directly transferable to behavioral anomaly detection
in proctoring . An interview is fundamentally a long-form temporal activity where “normal” behavior
(genuine candidate responses) has distinct spatiotemporal patterns compared to “anomalous” behavior
(reading from hidden scripts, receiving AI assistance, proxy interviewing). (Medium) The model’s ability
to capture long-range temporal dependencies — analyzing how a candidate’s gaze, posture, and
hand movements evolve over the full duration of an interview — provides detection capabilities that
frame-by-frame analysis cannot achieve.
The T emporal Patch Shift (TPS) method offers a plug-and-play enhancement that can be embedded
into existing 2D transformers without additional parameters or computation cost. (COMP’s Personal
W ebpage Server) TPS shifts patches from neighboring frames into the current frame before self-attention,
enabling the vanilla spatial attention module to perform spatiotemporal reasoning. On action recognition
datasets, TPS achieves 58.3% on Something-Something V1, 69.8% on V2, 82.5% on Kinetics-400, and
86.0% on Diving-48 — comparable to or better than the best T ransformer models with less computation.
(COMP’s Personal W ebpage Server) F or proctoring, this means existing 2D facial analysis networks can
be upgraded to temporal behavioral analysis with minimal architectural changes.
4. Deepfake Detection: CNN-T ransformer Architectures and the CAST
Model
4.1 The CAST Cross-Attention Architecture
The CAST (Cross-Attentive Spatio-T emporal) model represents the current state-of-the-art in
deepfake video detection, achieving an AUC of 99.49% and accuracy of 97.57% on F aceF orensics++
intra-dataset evaluation, with 93.31% AUC on the unseen DeepF akeDetection dataset in cross-dataset
testing. (arXiv.org) The core innovation is a unified cross-attention mechanism that enables tem-
poral features to dynamically attend to relevant spatial regions, enhancing detection of fine-grained
time-evolving artifacts such as flickering eyes, warped lips, and inconsistent facial textures. (arXiv.org)
The architecture processes 16 frames sampled at regular intervals from video clips. A CNN backbone
(EﬀicientNet-B0 or B5) extracts spatial features from each frame, producing feature maps that are pro-
jected via 1×1 convolution. These spatial features feed into a T ransformer encoder that models temporal
dependencies. The critical cross-attention block sits between the CNN and T ransformer: temporal
queries attend to spatial keys/ values , enabling temporal representations to selectively focus on
manipulation-prone spatial regions. (arXiv.org) The model’s output is averaged across frames and clas-
sified via sigmoid. Ablation studies confirm the cross-attention mechanism is essential — its removal
causes AUC to drop from 99.49% to 98.12% on intra-dataset testing and from 93.31% to 90.10% on
cross-dataset generalization. (arXiv.org)

4.3 The Challenge-Response Paradigm for Live Deepfake Detection
Research from UC Berkeley’s “Gotcha” system proposes a fundamentally different approach: instead
of passively analyzing video for deepfake artifacts, the system requires candidates to perform specific
challenges during live interactions that are diﬀicult for deepfake generation pipelines to model in real-
time. (arXiv.org) The core hypothesis is that each component in a deepfake pipeline has optimal operating
conditions, and any divergence compromises performance, leading to discernible artifacts. Challenges
include rapid facial movements (extreme expressions, rapid head turns), hand-over-face gestures
(occluding portions of the face), specific lighting changes (turning lights on/off), and simultaneous
speech and movement .(arXiv.org)
The research collected data from 47 real users performing eight distinct challenges , generating
56,247 short real and fake videos for training detection models. Both human and automated evalu-
ations demonstrated consistent and visible degradation of deepfake quality caused by challenges.
This paradigm shifts the security model from “detect deepfakes” to “make deepfake generation fail” — a
proactive rather than reactive approach. (arXiv.org) F or interview proctoring, this could be implemented
as periodic verification prompts during long interviews: “Please turn your head fully to the left, then to
the right” or “Please cover your mouth with your hand and say your name. ”
5. Continuous Authentication: The Deep Learning of Behavioral Biometrics
5.1 CNN-LSTM-Attention Architecture for Session-Long V erification
Continuous authentication verifies identity throughout an entire session rather than only at login, creating
abehavioral fingerprint that is nearly impossible to replicate. The most effective architecture combines
three deep learning components: a CNN for extracting local spatial features from raw sensor data
(keystroke timing, mouse movements, touch patterns), a Bi-LSTM for modeling forward-and-backward
temporal context across the session, and an Attention mechanism for learning which parts of the
sequence are most discriminative for user identification. (MDPI)
Experimental evaluation on a dataset of 45 participants using a mobile banking application achieved
99.85% accuracy with F AR (F alse Acceptance Rate), FRR (F alse Rejection Rate), and EER
(Equal Error Rate) all below 0.5% .(University of T wente Research Information) This performance
dramatically exceeds traditional machine learning approaches: Random F orest achieves only 93% accuracy
with 4% EER, while k-NN and logistic regression perform even worse. (MDPI) The attention mechanism
is particularly critical — without it, the model cannot learn which segments of intermittent or irregular
user activity are most relevant for identification, causing underperformance when users transition between
different activities (typing, scrolling, pausing). (MDPI)

5.2 Spiking Neural Networks and Edge Deployment
SNNAuth introduces an energy-eﬀicient alternative using Spiking Neural Networks for continuous
authentication on smartphone sensors. (MDPI) SNNs process information through discrete spike events
rather than continuous activations, making them orders of magnitude more energy-eﬀicient than
traditional deep networks. The framework extracts behavioral patterns from accelerometer and gyroscope
data, achieving high accuracy with minimal power consumption. However, SNNAuth is designed primarily
for binary classification (single legitimate user vs. impostors) rather than multi-user discrimination,
which limits its applicability in shared-device proctoring scenarios. (MDPI)
The IntelliAuth framework takes a broader approach by analyzing six daily activities (walking,
sitting, standing, running, walking up/down stairs) across five smartphone positions on the user’s
body , using time and frequency domain features classified through conventional algorithms. (MDPI)
While this achieves moderate accuracy , it relies on classical feature extraction rather than end-to-end
deep learning, limiting its ability to capture subtle behavioral patterns that CNNs and T ransformers can
learn automatically from raw data.

7. The Psychology of Interview Cheating: Why Candidates Cheat
7.1 The Three Drivers: Imposter Syndrome, Misaligned Expectations, and Normalization
Research published in the academic journal Ethics and Behavior reveals a critical insight: most candi-
dates who cheat do not initially recognize their acts as cheating .(CoderPad) While students
(and by extension, job candidates) generally believe cheating is wrong, they often judge the exceptional
cases in which they cheat to be acceptable, citing concerns about assignment goals, task feasibility , and
process fairness. This is not malicious deception — it is cognitive rationalization under pressure .
(CoderPad)
The data from a Reddit poll of candidates breaks down cheating motivations into three categories: 61%
never felt the need to cheat; 34% felt justified in cheating due to frustration with the interview process;
and only 5% cheated because they were simply unprepared. (CoderPad) The two root causes that
explain the 34% are: lack of confidence in skills (including imposter syndrome, where candidates
with adequate skills perceive themselves as incompetent) and misaligned expectations (where the
assessment tests skills irrelevant to the actual job or takes unreasonable time). (CoderPad)
The American Psychological Association reports that imposter syndrome triggers a fear of failure ,
leading candidates to self-sabotage by underperforming in interviews or withdrawing from competitive
applications. (CaseBasix) This creates a vicious cycle: candidates who experience imposter anxiety feel
they cannot succeed honestly , which increases the temptation to use unauthorized assistance, which if
detected destroys their candidacy , which further reinforces their imposter beliefs. The “superhuman
imposter syndrome” subtype — where individuals feel they must excel in every role to prove their worth
— is particularly prevalent in high-pressure tech hiring cultures and directly fuels cheating behavior.
(CaseBasix)
7.2 The Cheating Culture F eedback Loop
Academic research on student misconduct provides a directly applicable framework for understanding
how cheating becomes normalized in hiring. When candidates successfully cheat without detection, the
behavior becomes socially normalized within peer networks. (nih.gov) If there is a belief that cheating
is commonplace, a “cheating culture” develops that makes it more probable that current and future
candidates will engage in misconduct. The phenomenon is reinforced on platforms like T eam Blind and
Reddit, where candidates openly discuss cheating strategies, share tools like Cluely , and frame cheating
as a rational response to an unfair system. (Blind)

10.1 The Privacy Challenge in Centralized Proctoring
T raditional proctoring architectures require transmitting candidate video, audio, and biometric data to
centralized servers for analysis. This creates significant privacy risks : data breaches expose sensitive
biometric information; cross-border data transfers trigger GDPR compliance requirements; and candi-
dates rightfully resist having their facial data stored on third-party servers. The EU AI Act’s high-risk

F ederated Learning (FL) offers a privacy-preserving alternative by enabling distributed model train-
ing across heterogeneous devices without sharing raw data .(Springer) In the proctoring context, this
means the detection models can be trained on behavioral patterns from thousands of interviews without
any video or biometric data leaving the candidate’s device. Only model updates (gradients) are transmit-
ted to a central aggregation server, and these updates can be further protected via differential privacy ,
secure multi-party computation (SMPC), or homomorphic encryption. (Springer)
10.2 Edge Computing for Real-Time Inference
Edge computing enhances this architecture by performing inference directly on the candidate’s device
rather than in the cloud. The HFEL (Hierarchical F ederated Edge Learning) framework demonstrates
how this works in practice: lightweight detection models run on-device using T ensorFlow Lite or ONNX
Runtime, processing video and audio streams locally and transmitting only anomaly alerts (not raw
data) to the proctoring server. (Springer) This reduces latency from hundreds of milliseconds to <50ms ,
eliminates network bandwidth constraints, and ensures that sensitive biometric data never leaves the
device.
The security architecture for federated proctoring must address several attack vectors: man-in-the-
middle attacks on model updates (countered via TLS 1.3 with Perfect F orward Secrecy), Sybil attacks
where malicious clients submit fake updates (countered via cosine similarity-based anomaly detection),
model poisoning where adversarial updates corrupt the global model (countered via gradient clipping
and z-score outlier detection), and replay attacks (countered via timestamped communications and
nonces). (Springer) SHA-256 hashing with ECDSA signatures ensures data integrity and authenticity at
both cluster and global levels. When additional confidentiality is required, SMPC enables aggregation
without exposing individual model parameters. (Springer)

11.2 Counter-Countermeasures and the F uture Arms Race
The detection arms race follows a predictable escalation pattern. As detection systems improve, cheating
tools evolve to evade them: when gaze tracking detects reading patterns, tools add saccade simulation
(synthetic eye movements); when audio analysis detects unnatural pauses, tools implement response
jitter (variable timing); when keystroke dynamics detect bot patterns, tools add humanized typing
(realistic error injection and correction). (Harjai AI) The fundamental asymmetry favors cheaters be-
cause they only need to evade one detection signal, while detection systems must monitor all signals
simultaneously .
The most promising counter-countermeasure is behavioral consistency modeling using deep learning.
Rather than flagging individual anomalies, these models build a comprehensive behavioral profile of
the candidate across the entire interview and flag statistically improbable patterns. (F abric) F or example,
a candidate who normally types at 45 WPM with 8% error rate but suddenly produces perfect code at
120 WPM creates a multivariate anomaly that is extremely unlikely to be explained by normal variation.
Similarly , a candidate whose vocabulary complexity score jumps from the 40th percentile to the 95th
percentile between questions triggers a coherence alert. These consistency models are harder to evade
because they require the cheater to maintain realistic behavior across all dimensions simultaneously
for the entire duration of the interview — a significantly higher bar than evading any single detection
signal.

12.2 Layer 2: Behavioral Monitoring (Detection)
The second layer uses the multimodal fusion architecture described in Section 9 to detect cheating in
real-time. This layer operates continuously throughout the interview, scoring each moment on an au-
thenticity probability scale from 0 (certain cheating) to 1 (certain genuine). Scores below 0.3 trigger
immediate human reviewer alerts; scores between 0.3 and 0.7 are flagged for post-interview review; scores
above 0.7 are considered normal. (F abric)
12.3 Layer 3: Content Strategy (Adaptation)
The third layer uses adaptive questioning to make cheating ineffective regardless of the tools used.
When a candidate provides a suspiciously perfect answer, the interviewer (human or AI) immediately
drills deeper with context-specific follow-ups that LLMs struggle to answer coherently . T echniques in-
clude: asking about non-existent technologies (LLMs hallucinate plausible-sounding but fake meth-
ods), rapid context switching between unrelated domains (breaking coherence), questions with no
correct answer (testing intellectual honesty), and requiring line-by-line code explanation (forcing
genuine understanding). (F abric)
12.4 Layer 4: Human V erification (V alidation)
The fourth layer ensures that no candidate is rejected based solely on AI detection. Human review of
flagged segments by trained proctoring analysts provides the final determination. F ollow-up inter-
views (either live video or in-person) with different questions verify candidate capabilities. Reference
checks and background verification catch proxy interviewing and credential fraud. This human-in-
the-loop requirement is not just best practice — it is legally mandated under the EU AI Act (high-risk
systems must include human oversight) and NYC Local Law 144 (candidates can request alternative
evaluation processes). (Employsome)

Behavioral consistency models are the most resilient defense. While individual signals can be

simultaneously for an entire interview is orders of magnitude harder than evading any single detection
mechanism. (F abric)
